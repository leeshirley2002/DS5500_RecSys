\begin{abstract}
Purchase histories in retail recommendation settings are often short and sparse, so models that rely only on past purchases may miss products a user is currently considering. This report analyzes the cart-to-purchase signal on the Synerise RecSys~2025 active-user cohort. We find that 24.84\% of carted user--SKU pairs later convert to a purchase, 81.71\% of purchased pairs had a prior same-SKU cart-add, and the median cart-to-purchase lag is approximately 11~minutes. These results support treating cart history as an informative and timely intent signal, while incomplete conversion and remove-from-cart patterns caution against treating cart-adds as hard positives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Purchase-based recommenders can under-represent current intent because purchases are infrequent, whereas add-to-cart events are more frequent and may reflect more recent consideration. This exploratory analysis studies the cart-to-purchase signal on the Synerise RecSys~2025 active-user cohort---the analysis that most directly motivates the project hypothesis---by asking how often adding an item to the cart leads to a later purchase of the same item by the same user, and how soon after the first cart-add that purchase tends to occur. If conversion is material and many conversions occur in a short window, cart history is both informative and timely relative to sparse purchase histories, supporting cart-augmented sequential recommenders over purchase-only baselines.

\section{Methods}
We analyze three event files from the Step~1 active-user subsample (users with $K \geq 5$ total purchase/cart events) over the window 2022-06-23 to 2022-12-08: 4,890,231 add-to-cart events (510,728 users; 1,069,877 SKUs), 1,171,501 purchases (293,044 users; 449,958 SKUs), and 2,229,315 remove-from-cart events (423,386 users; 741,269 SKUs). Records were deduplicated and timestamps parsed. Because the cohort retains more active shoppers, findings may not generalize to the full Synerise population.

The primary unit is the unique \texttt{(client\_id, sku)} pair. For each pair we take the earliest cart-add and the earliest purchase; a pair is a conversion if a purchase of that SKU occurs strictly after the first cart-add, matching the project's preliminary EDA definition and excluding purchases that precede the cart signal. We report the pair-level conversion rate, the share of purchased pairs with a prior same-SKU cart-add, the user-level conversion rate among users with at least one cart event, and conversion stratified by whether an intervening remove-from-cart occurred after the first cart and before any subsequent purchase. For converted pairs we also compute $\mathrm{gap}=t_{\mathrm{first\ purchase}}-t_{\mathrm{first\ cart}}$ and summarize its distribution with percentiles, cumulative windows from one hour to 30~days, and coarse timing buckets. Pair-level first-cart to first-later-purchase is an aggregate intent measure rather than a session-level probability, and the release provides no session IDs, so timing reflects calendar elapsed time; category stratification is out of scope here.

\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:conversion} shows that 878,617 of 3,537,047 carted user--SKU pairs subsequently convert, a pair-level rate of 24.84\%. Among purchased pairs, 81.71\% had a prior same-SKU cart-add, and 52.72\% of users with cart activity convert at least once. Remove-from-cart is associated with much weaker follow-through: pairs with no intervening remove convert at 35.73\%, compared with 11.22\% for pairs removed before purchase or never purchased after a remove (Table~\ref{tab:remove}).

Among 878,617 converted pairs with a positive gap, the lag distribution is heavily right-skewed (Table~\ref{tab:gap}): the median is 0.18~hours (about 11~minutes), while the mean is 31.65~hours because of a long delayed tail. Most conversions occur quickly (Table~\ref{tab:windows}): 72.13\% of eventual converters purchase within one hour, 86.52\% within 24~hours, and 95.49\% within seven days. Among converters, 72.1\% fall in the 0--1h bucket, 14.4\% in 1--24h, 9.0\% in 1--7d, 3.6\% in 7--30d, and 0.9\% beyond 30~days.

\section{Discussion}
A pair-level conversion rate of about 25\% indicates that cart history carries non-trivial information about what a user may buy next, and the finding that 81.71\% of purchased pairs had a prior same-SKU cart-add shows that cart is often an observable precursor of purchase. Treating cart events as noise relative to purchase-only baselines would therefore discard a large share of purchases that are preceded by cart activity. At the same time, conversion is typically near-term: the median lag is about 11~minutes and roughly 87\% of converters buy within a day, so cart events are often more recent than older purchases in sparse histories and can supply fresher intent than purchase history alone.

Incomplete conversion remains important. About 75\% of carted pairs never convert, and an intervening remove is associated with much lower conversion (11.22\% versus 35.73\%), so cart-adds should not be treated as guaranteed future purchases. Multi-behavior models should encode event type---buy, add, and remove---and keep removes and non-conversions as weak or negative signals rather than collapsing all interactions into one positive class. These results motivate holding architecture fixed (for example, SASRec) and comparing purchase-only sequences with cart-augmented sequences under a temporal split; whether cart usefulness varies by anonymized category is left to separate modeling. Limitations include restriction to the $K\geq 5$ cohort, first-event pairing that ignores repeated carting dynamics, calendar rather than session timing, and the observational (non-causal) nature of the remove-versus-conversion association.

\section{Conclusion}
On the Synerise RecSys~2025 active-user cohort, cart-to-purchase behavior supports the project hypothesis: approximately 24.84\% of carted user--SKU pairs later purchase the same item, approximately 81.71\% of purchased pairs had a prior same-SKU cart-add, and the median cart-to-purchase lag is about 11~minutes, with roughly 87\% of converters buying within 24~hours and 95\% within seven days. Cart history is therefore an informative and timely signal of purchase intent, while incomplete conversion and remove-from-cart patterns caution against treating cart-adds as hard positives. The appropriate next step is a controlled comparison of purchase-only versus cart-augmented sequential recommenders under a temporal evaluation split.
